What is food made of? Macro- and micronutrients

Updated: July 2026
Macronutrients Energy Micronutrients Recommendations Practical tips References

What is actually in our food, and why does our body need it? Nutrition provides the building blocks and the energy we need to function, from every heartbeat to every thought. Yet there is no universal dietary pattern that is optimal for everyone: genetics, activity level, health status and life stage all help determine what a person needs. This article lays the foundation. What are macronutrients, what are micronutrients, and what do scientific guidelines say about recommended amounts?

Macronutrients

Macronutrients are the three main components of food that provide energy: carbohydrates, proteins and fats. A fourth source of energy is alcohol, although it is not an essential nutrient. The Dutch Health Council (2001) sets dietary reference values for each of these substances, based on physiological needs and the available scientific evidence.

Carbohydrates are the primary fuel for the brain and muscles. Wholegrain varieties such as wholemeal bread, oats and pulses also provide fibre that supports gut health and stabilises blood sugar levels. The WHO/FAO (2003) recommends that 55 to 75% of total energy intake come from carbohydrates, with an emphasis on unprocessed sources.

Proteins are essential for muscle repair, cell renewal and the production of enzymes and hormones. The Dutch Health Council (2021) advises 0.83 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day for adults; athletes and older people generally need more, around 1.2 to 1.6 g/kg. Good sources are pulses, dairy, eggs, fish and meat.

Fats are indispensable for building cell membranes, producing hormones and absorbing fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K). The type of fat is decisive: unsaturated fats from olive oil, nuts and oily fish are beneficial for cardiovascular health, while trans fats and an excess of saturated fats raise LDL cholesterol (EFSA, 2017).

Energy and calories

Energy intake and expenditure together determine the energy balance. With a positive balance, more intake than expenditure, energy is stored as glycogen or fat tissue. With a negative balance, the body draws on these reserves.

Total energy requirement consists of three components: basal metabolism (around 60 to 70%), the thermic effect of food (around 10%) and physical activity (variable). Trumbo et al. (2002) describe how the calculation of energy requirement differs per person based on age, sex, weight, height and activity level. Online calculation methods such as the Harris-Benedict formula give a rough estimate; a dietitian calculates this more precisely based on your situation.

Micronutrients

Micronutrients, the vitamins and minerals, provide no direct energy, but are indispensable for virtually all physiological processes: from energy production and immune function to bone building and nerve conduction. Deficiencies are possible even with a seemingly varied diet, particularly for vitamin D, iodine, iron and folate (EFSA, 2017).

Recommended amounts

The Dutch Health Council sets Recommended Daily Amounts (RDA) per age group, sex and physiological state such as pregnancy or lactation. These values are based on the amount sufficient for 97.5% of the healthy population. EFSA publishes comparable Dietary Reference Values at the European level.

Important: RDA values are population norms, not personal targets. In the case of chronic conditions, intensive sport, a vegetarian or vegan diet or specific health complaints, individual needs can differ considerably. A dietitian calculates what your body specifically needs.

Practical guidelines

  • Eat varied: variety increases the chance of getting all micro- and macronutrients
  • Choose wholegrain over refined more often: more fibre, vitamins and minerals per serving
  • Eat fish regularly, including oily fish, for omega-3 and vitamin D
  • Limit meat to a maximum of 300 grams per week, of which no more than 100 grams red meat (Netherlands Nutrition Centre, Wheel of Five)
  • Use iodised salt in moderation; iodine deficiency is a real risk in the Netherlands
  • Consider vitamin D supplementation in winter, especially if you spend little time outdoors
  • Vegans: pay attention to B12, calcium, iron, zinc and omega-3; supplementation or fortified products are necessary

Nutrition is therefore not a matter of isolated pills or a few 'superfoods', but of a coherent whole of building blocks that your body needs every day. At Phrona we translate these general norms into what works for you personally, because ideal nutrition looks slightly different for everyone.
