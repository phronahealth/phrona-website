Habit formation and behaviour change

Updated: July 2026

Why is it so hard to keep up healthy behaviour, even when we know exactly what is good for us? The answer lies largely in the way our brain forms habits. Habits are not a matter of willpower or character, but the result of a biological learning process that automates behaviour. Anyone who understands how that process works can change more effectively and more durably.

What exactly is a habit

A habit is a learned response that is triggered automatically by a recurring context, without any conscious deliberation. Wood and Neal (2007) describe habits as associations between a situation (the 'cue' or trigger) and a behaviour, which through repetition become so strong that the cue alone is enough to set the behaviour in motion. Think of automatically reaching for your phone the moment you are in a queue, or opening the fridge as you walk into the kitchen.

The key insight is that habits arise from repetition in a stable context, not from motivation or good intentions. Behaviour that is initially goal-directed ('I want to lose weight, so I take the stairs') turns, with enough repetition, into an automatism that is independent of the original goal. That explains why habits persist even when the motivation has faded.

The cue-routine-reward model

Habitual behaviour revolves around a loop of three elements: a trigger, the behaviour itself and a reward. The reward strengthens the association: the more consistently a particular behaviour is rewarded in a particular context, the stronger the tendency to repeat it. At the neurological level, control gradually shifts from the prefrontal cortex, responsible for conscious planning and self-control, to the basal ganglia, deeper in the brain, which drive automated behaviour (Wood & Neal, 2007).

This shift is functional: automation saves mental energy. The downside is that unhealthy habits are laid down in exactly the same way, and that the trigger keeps evoking the behaviour even when we consciously no longer want it.

How long does it take to form a habit

The popular idea that a habit is formed in 21 days lacks scientific support; it stems from an observation by a plastic surgeon in the 1960s. Lally et al. (2010) studied habit formation in real life and found an average of 66 days before a new behaviour felt automatic. The range, however, was enormous: from 18 to 254 days, depending on the person and the complexity of the behaviour.

A systematic review and meta-analysis recently confirmed this picture: most health habits need two to five months to automate, with an individual range of a few days to almost a year (Singh et al., 2024). Simple behaviours (a glass of water at breakfast) automate faster than complex ones (half an hour of exercise every day).

What works according to the science

The same research points to a number of consistent principles:

  • Consistency matters more than perfection. Lally et al. (2010) found that missing a single day does not meaningfully disrupt the process. Repeatedly skipping does.
  • Link new behaviour to a fixed trigger. Using an existing routine as an anchor ('after brushing my teeth', 'as soon as I make coffee') speeds up automation because the context is stable.
  • Start small. Small, achievable behaviours are repeated more often and therefore automate faster than ambitious goals that demand a lot of willpower.
  • Change the environment, not just yourself. Because habits are triggered by context, adjusting your environment (unhealthy snacks out of sight, sports clothes laid out ready) is often more effective than more self-discipline.
  • Allow time. Realistic expectations, two to five months instead of three weeks, prevent people from giving up too early.

Breaking old habits

Existing habits cannot simply be erased; the underlying cue-behaviour association remains present in the brain. More effective than 'unlearning' is replacing the behaviour: linking the same trigger to a different, more desirable behaviour. It also helps to avoid or change triggers. Moments of change in your surroundings, such as moving house or a new job, offer a natural window because the old context, and with it the old triggers, temporarily falls away (Wood & Neal, 2007).

Practical tips

  • Choose one concrete, small behaviour at a time rather than several big changes at once
  • Link it to a fixed moment or an existing routine as a trigger
  • Make the desired behaviour as easy as possible and the unwanted behaviour as hard as possible through your environment
  • Keep going, even after a missed day; skipping once is not a failure
  • Give yourself at least two to three months before you expect something to become automatic
  • When breaking a habit, focus on replacing, not forbidding

Behaviour change is not a matter of more motivation, but of making smart use of how the brain learns.
