Brain and nutrition

Updated: July 2026

Why do we reach for crisps when we are tired or stressed, even when we have just eaten? Why does the craving for something sweet sometimes feel stronger than any good intention? The answer lies in our brain. Our brain accounts for roughly 2% of our body weight but consumes around 20% of our total energy needs. What we eat therefore directly influences how it functions: concentration, mood, memory and appetite. That relationship between nutrition and the brain is more complex than often assumed and is driven by an interplay of hormones, neurotransmitters and deeply rooted evolutionary mechanisms.

How the brain steers food choices

The primary function of food is to supply fuel and building blocks. When the stomach empties, the hormone ghrelin, the 'hunger hormone', rises and activates brain regions that drive motivation and reward behaviour (Cummings et al., 2002). That hunger is not a weakness or a lack of willpower: it is a strongly conserved survival mechanism that is older than humanity itself.

Berthoud (2011) describes how two systems in the brain are constantly interacting. The homeostatic system (the hypothalamus) regulates energy balance based on physiological needs, while the hedonic system, primarily the reward circuit, responds to taste, smell and the anticipation of pleasure. In an environment of food abundance, the hedonic system regularly wins, even when we objectively need no energy at all.

Dopamine and food reward

Foods rich in fat, sugar and salt activate the dopaminergic reward system more strongly than unprocessed foods (Volkow et al., 2011). Dopamine is released both in anticipation of and during the consumption of palatable food and reinforces habitual behaviour: the more often a particular food is eaten in a particular context, the stronger the drive to repeat it.

This explains why ultra-processed food is so hard to resist. These products are specifically engineered to maximise exactly those combinations of fat, sugar and salt. It is therefore not a lack of discipline, but a physiological system responding to products that simply did not exist in our evolutionary history.

Environment as a determining factor

Food choices are largely determined by the environment, not by conscious decisions. Story et al. (2008) introduced the 'ecological model' of eating behaviour: individual factors such as knowledge, preference and habits are often overshadowed by social and physical environmental factors such as availability, portion size, social norms and marketing.

Anyone surrounded by calorie-dense snacks at home or at work eats more of them, regardless of intention. Conversely, simply making fruit and vegetables visible and available significantly increases their consumption. This means that behaviour change is more effective when it occurs at the environmental level, and does not rely on individual willpower alone.

Knowledge as a counterweight

Although environment and biology carry significant weight, knowledge is anything but irrelevant. Lieberman (2013) argues that it is precisely the uniquely human capacity for cultural transmission, the ability to change behaviour based on information, that is our most powerful tool for coping with the evolutionary mismatch. Understanding why certain food choices arise enables the more conscious construction of alternative habits.

Practical tips

  • Eat at consistent times to limit ghrelin spikes and impulsive choices
  • Adjust your environment: keep unhealthy snacks out of sight and put fruit on the table
  • Eat mindfully and without distractions such as phone or TV; this strengthens satiety signals
  • Understand that cravings for sweet or fatty foods are biology, not weakness, and plan ahead
  • Choose varied, minimally processed food as a base; this supports a more stable reward system

Eating behaviour is therefore not a simple sum of willpower and good intentions, but the result of hormones, reward and environment together steering our behaviour.
