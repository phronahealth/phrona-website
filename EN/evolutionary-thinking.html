Evolutionary thinking

Updated: July 2026
Nutrition Movement Sleep Stress Social connection Nature Practical tips References

Why are lifestyle diseases such as obesity, diabetes and cardiovascular disease becoming ever more common, precisely now that we have more knowledge, food and comfort than ever? A large part of the answer lies not in our behaviour today, but in our distant prehistory. Our body was shaped by millions of years of evolution in an environment radically different from the world we live in now. Evolutionary thinking uses that insight as a lens: it helps us understand why our body responds the way it does, and why the modern environment so often works against us.

Lieberman (2013) describes this as a 'mismatch': the biological basis of our body is still largely calibrated for a hunter-gatherer world, while we live in a world of supermarkets, screens and a 24-hour economy. That gap between what we were designed for and how we now live explains a large part of our modern health complaints (Wells, 2017). Below we walk through six areas where this mismatch is most clearly visible.

Nutrition: built for scarcity, living in abundance

For most of human evolution, food was scarce and seasonal. Our brain and metabolism evolved to actively seek out and store calorie-dense food, rich in fat, sugar and salt. That adaptation was lifesaving in times of scarcity, but backfires in an environment of permanent abundance (Lieberman, 2013). The craving for a fatty snack is therefore not a character flaw, but an ancient survival mechanism that has ended up in the wrong environment.

Ultra-processed food amplifies this mechanism further. Monteiro et al. (2019) showed that these products account for an ever-growing share of caloric intake worldwide and are strongly associated with obesity, type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease. Conversely, Satija et al. (2017) demonstrated that plant-based dietary patterns, rich in unprocessed products, significantly reduce the risk of chronic disease.

Movement: a body not designed to sit still

Hunter-gatherers walked an average of nine to fifteen kilometres a day; modern office work demands hardly any physical exertion. Lieberman (2013) calls prolonged sitting an 'evolutionarily novel behaviour' for which the body simply was not built. Chronic sitting increases the risk of metabolic disorders, and this even applies to people who exercise regularly on the side (Ekelund et al., 2019).

The Dutch Health Council (2017) recommends at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per week, supplemented with muscle-strengthening activities. But small interruptions to sitting matter too: standing or walking for a moment every thirty minutes already has measurable health benefits. It is therefore not only about intensive exercise, but above all about less prolonged, unbroken sitting.

Sleep: living against the clock

Our circadian rhythm, the internal 24-hour clock, regulates not only sleeping and waking but also hormone release, metabolism and immune function. Artificial light in the evening, irregular working hours and screen use chronically disrupt that rhythm. Czeisler (2013) describes sleep deprivation as one of the most underestimated public-health problems of our time: it increases the risk of obesity, diabetes, depression and cardiovascular disease.

In evolutionary terms, humans slept in synchrony with the light: awake at sunrise, asleep after sunset. Returning fully to that rhythm is unrealistic in the modern world, but consistent sleep times, a dark bedroom and limiting blue light in the evening already help considerably to support that ancient clock.

Stress: an alarm system that never switches off

The physiological stress response, the 'fight-or-flight' system, evolved to deal with acute danger: a predator, a threat, a fight. Short-term stress is functional and even protective. The problem is that chronic psychosocial stress, such as work pressure, financial worries or social isolation, activates exactly the same systems continuously, but without the physical discharge they were meant for (McEwen, 2007).

This permanent activation leads to elevated cortisol levels, inflammatory responses and a greater risk of cardiovascular disease, depression and metabolic syndrome. Proven effective counterweights align with our biology: regular physical activity, social support, sufficient sleep and attention training such as mindfulness.

Social connection: we survived together

Humans are ultrasocial animals. Social bonding was vital in evolutionary terms: hunting together, caring for children, offering protection against danger. Those who fell outside the group rarely survived. Holt-Lunstad et al. (2010) analysed 148 studies and concluded that strong social relationships lower the risk of premature death by roughly 50%, an effect comparable to quitting smoking.

Loneliness is therefore increasingly recognised as a serious health risk, not merely an emotional discomfort. Maintaining meaningful relationships, taking part in shared activities and investing in your social network are therefore not a luxury, but a basic condition for health.

Nature and environment: out of balance with our origins

Humans spent almost all of their evolution outdoors, in direct interaction with nature. Twohig-Bennett and Jones (2018) showed in a systematic review that exposure to green environments is associated with lower cortisol levels, lower blood pressure, less anxiety and better overall health.

Urbanisation strongly limits that exposure, but relatively simple measures are already effective: walking in a park, working by a window with a view of greenery, or regularly spending time outside the city. Our body still recognises nature as home.

Practical application

  • Choose unprocessed, varied food as much as possible and limit ultra-processed products
  • Interrupt sedentary work every thirty minutes with a short movement break
  • Keep fixed sleep and wake times and limit screen use in the evening
  • Build in a daily moment of relaxation: walking, breathing or meditation
  • Invest actively in social relationships; quality counts more than quantity
  • Seek out nature regularly, even if it is just a city park

Evolutionary thinking is not a nostalgic plea for a return to the Stone Age, even though that is a popular narrative on social media. It is a scientific framework that helps us understand why certain lifestyle choices benefit our body. At Phrona we use that insight not to impose strict rules, but to make choices together with you that fit how your body is truly designed, within the demands of modern life.
