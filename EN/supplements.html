Supplements: what works, what doesn't, and what you should know

Updated: July 2026

The market for dietary supplements is huge and growing fast. Vitamin pills, fish oil, protein powders and countless 'superfoods' are promoted as the key to health and performance. But the scientific evidence varies strongly per product. For most healthy people with a varied diet, the majority of supplements are unnecessary, while a small number are genuinely useful or even essential in specific situations.

To weigh those claims properly, it helps to know how large the interests at stake are. The global health-and-wellness industry, of which dietary supplements are a fast-growing part, is estimated at more than 6.8 trillion dollars, making it more than three times the size of the pharmaceutical industry (Global Wellness Institute, 2024; IQVIA, 2024). Where sums like that are involved, healthy scepticism is warranted. A large share of the supplement advice people receive comes from parties who also sell the products themselves. Anyone who advises and sells at the same time has a direct financial interest that can conflict with the scientific evidence, and that evidence shows that most supplements add little or nothing for healthy people (USPSTF, 2022; Guallar et al., 2013). Professional bodies such as the American Medical Association therefore explicitly regard the sale of supplements by healthcare providers as a conflict of interest (AMA, Code of Medical Ethics 9.6.4). So be extra critical when a nutrition expert or dietitian recommends a supplement that you can then buy from that same person.

Food first, supplements as an addition

The starting point of virtually all nutritional guidelines is clear: a varied, minimally processed diet in principle provides all the necessary nutrients. Supplements are intended as an addition in the case of a demonstrated deficiency or an increased need, not as a replacement for healthy food. A pill does not compensate for an unhealthy diet: the health effects of whole foods arise from a complex interplay of substances that an isolated supplement cannot mimic.

Supplements with clear evidence

Vitamin D is the best-known example of useful supplementation in the Netherlands. Our skin produces vitamin D under the influence of sunlight, but in the winter months (roughly October to April) the sun is too low for sufficient production. The Health Council therefore advises specific groups to take a daily supplement: among others people over 70 (20 micrograms per day), young children, pregnant women, people with darker skin and people who spend little time outdoors (10 micrograms per day) (Health Council, 2012).

Vitamin B12 is essential and occurs almost exclusively in animal products. Anyone who eats a vegan or predominantly plant-based diet must supplement B12 or use fortified products; a deficiency can lead to anaemia and irreversible neurological damage.

Folic acid is recommended for women wishing to conceive and in the first weeks of pregnancy, to prevent neural tube defects in the child.

Omega-3 fatty acids from oily fish have a beneficial effect on cardiovascular health. For people who eat little or no fish, a supplement can be a reasonable addition. Meta-analyses show a modest but real effect on cardiovascular outcomes, with EPA-rich supplements in particular appearing beneficial (Khan et al., 2021). The effects are, however, smaller than marketing often suggests.

Supplements where the evidence often disappoints

For many popular supplements the evidence is weak or absent. Multivitamins offer no demonstrable health benefit in healthy, well-nourished people and do not lower the risk of chronic disease. Antioxidant supplements (such as high doses of vitamin C or E) turn out to be ineffective in trials at preventing cardiovascular disease or cancer, and in high doses can even be harmful. For most 'slimming' and 'detox' supplements, convincing evidence is likewise lacking.

Protein powders are a special case: they are not harmful and can be practical for topping up protein intake, but offer no advantage over protein from ordinary food. For most people it is simple enough to meet protein needs through diet.

Risks and misconceptions

More is not better. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) accumulate in the body and can be harmful in overdose. For vitamin D, for example, a safe upper limit of 100 micrograms per day applies for adults (Health Council, 2012). In addition, supplements can interact with medication.

It is also important to realise that the supplement industry relies heavily on marketing. As with processed food, health claims are deployed strategically, while the actual efficacy has often been little researched. A claim on the packaging is no proof of effect.

Practical tips

  • Base your diet on varied, minimally processed food; supplements are an addition, not a replacement
  • Consider vitamin D in the winter months, especially if you belong to a risk group
  • Vegans and predominantly plant-based eaters: supplement B12 and watch iron, calcium and omega-3
  • Women wishing to conceive: take folic acid as advised
  • Be critical of health claims and 'detox' or slimming products
  • Never exceed the safe upper limit and consult your general practitioner (GP) if you take medication

Supplements can be valuable in specific situations, but for most healthy people they are not a necessity.
