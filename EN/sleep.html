Sleep: why it matters as much as nutrition and exercise

Updated: July 2026

Sleep is often seen as lost time, a period in which the body does nothing. In reality, sleep is an active and essential process in which the brain recovers, consolidates memories and regulates metabolism. Chronic sleep deprivation is among the most underestimated health risks of our time and is associated with an increased risk of obesity, diabetes, depression and cardiovascular disease (Czeisler, 2013).

The circadian rhythm

Our internal 24-hour clock, the circadian rhythm, governs not only sleeping and waking but also hormone release, body temperature, digestion and immune function. This rhythm is regulated mainly by light: morning light switches the clock 'on', while darkness triggers the production of the sleep hormone melatonin.

Artificial light in the evening, irregular working hours and screen use chronically disrupt this rhythm. The blue light of phones and screens suppresses melatonin production and delays falling asleep. In evolutionary terms, humans slept in synchrony with the light: awake at sunrise, asleep after sunset. Returning fully to that rhythm is unrealistic, but consistent sleep times and limiting light in the evening help considerably.

What happens during sleep

Sleep proceeds in cycles of about 90 minutes, in which deep (non-REM) sleep and dream sleep (REM) alternate. Both phases have their own functions. During deep non-REM sleep, mainly in the first half of the night, factual memories are consolidated: information moves from the hippocampus, the temporary memory, to the cortex for long-term storage (Walker, 2017). During this phase the brain also clears out waste products via the so-called glymphatic system.

REM sleep, concentrated in the second half of the night, processes emotional experiences and supports creativity and problem-solving. Those who sleep too little lose proportionally much of precisely this REM sleep. Both phases are indispensable and cannot replace one another.

Sleep, hunger and weight

Sleep and eating behaviour are more closely connected than is often thought. In a controlled experiment, sleep restriction in healthy young men led to a drop in the satiety hormone leptin and a rise in the hunger hormone ghrelin, resulting in more hunger and appetite, especially for calorie-dense and carbohydrate-rich foods (Spiegel et al., 2004). This hormonal shift can occur after just one or two nights of poor sleep.

This explains why insufficient sleep hampers weight loss and encourages overeating. Sleep is therefore not a separate topic, but a direct factor in eating behaviour and weight management. Anyone working on healthier eating without attention to sleep is missing an important piece of the puzzle.

Sleep and recovery

For anyone who exercises or is recovering from injury or illness, sleep is indispensable. During deep sleep, growth hormone is released, which is involved in muscle repair and tissue building. Sleep deprivation slows recovery, increases the risk of injury and reduces athletic performance. Cognitive performance too, concentration, reaction speed and decision-making, suffer measurably from a bad night.

How much sleep is enough

For most adults the optimal sleep duration lies between seven and nine hours per night. Individual needs vary, but structurally sleeping less than seven hours is consistently associated with health risks in population studies. What matters is not only the duration but also the regularity: going to bed and getting up at fixed times supports the circadian rhythm more strongly than the occasional long night to catch up on deficits.

Practical tips

  • Keep fixed sleep and wake times, including at weekends, to keep your internal clock stable
  • Seek daylight in the morning; this sets your circadian rhythm correctly
  • Limit screen use and bright light in the hour before bedtime
  • Ensure a dark, cool and quiet bedroom
  • Avoid caffeine in the afternoon and evening, and limit alcohol; both disrupt sleep quality
  • See sleep as part of your nutrition and exercise routine, not as an afterthought

Sleep is not a luxury, but a basic requirement for physical and mental health, and an indispensable partner of good nutrition and sufficient exercise. At Phrona we therefore look at the whole picture: how nutrition, exercise and sleep together contribute to durable recovery and well-being.
